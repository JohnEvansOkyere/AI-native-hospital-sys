{\LARGE\bfseries Beyond Word Error Rate: Task-Success Benchmarking of
Code-Switched Twi--English and Pidgin--English Speech for a Clinical Agent\par}
\vspace{0.4cm}
{\large John Evans Okyere\par}
\vspace{0.12cm}
{Veloxa Technology Limited, Accra, Ghana\par}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.4cm}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Speech recognition for African languages is normally reported as word error rate
(WER). In a deployed clinical agent, what matters is not the transcript but the
action it produces. We benchmark code-switched Ghanaian clinical speech ---
Twi--English and Pidgin--English --- on downstream task success: blood-pressure
extraction, escalation correctness under a fixed clinical rule, and intent. The
harness imports the production speech layer of a deployed WhatsApp chronic-care
agent, so the systems measured are the systems serving patients. Across an
African-built API (Intron Sahara, in two language-hint configurations), a
commercial API (Cartesia Ink) and an offline open-weights model
(faster-whisper), we find that \emph{the language hint given to a model changes
downstream accuracy more than the choice of model does}, and that its effect is
opposite in the two Ghanaian languages: the \texttt{pcm} hint makes Sahara the
strongest system on Pidgin--English by a wide margin (WER 0.085, intent 93\%),
while the \texttt{tw} hint returns empty transcripts on 16 of 27 Twi--English
utterances and drives escalation correctness to zero. Pinning the same API to
English instead recovers 78\% on Twi--English. We report escalation failures by
direction, since a missed escalation and a false alarm are not equivalent harms.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Index Terms}: speech recognition, code-switching, low-resource

\section{Introduction}

In a deployed clinical agent no clinician reads the transcript. What reaches a
human is an \emph{action}: a blood-pressure reading recorded against a patient,
an escalation raised to a nurse, a refill routed to a pharmacy. A benchmark that
stops at WER measures the intermediate artefact, not the thing the system is for.

This matters acutely in West African clinical speech, which is routinely
code-switched. Ghanaian patients mix Twi or Ghanaian Pidgin with English, and the
clinically load-bearing tokens --- numerals and drug names --- are almost always
spoken in English inside an otherwise non-English utterance. A model can
therefore mis-transcribe most of a sentence and still produce the correct action,
or transcribe most of it correctly and destroy the action.

We contribute a task-success harness that scores blood-pressure extraction,
escalation correctness and intent alongside WER and CER, and which \emph{imports
the production speech layer} rather than reimplementing it; a consented,
de-identified corpus of Ghanaian code-switched clinical utterances; and a
four-configuration comparison spanning an African-built API, a commercial API and
an open-weights model running fully offline on CPU.

\section{System and task}

VeloxaCare receives patient messages over WhatsApp as text or voice notes. A
speech layer transcribes voice; a rule-based component then extracts structured
content and decides what happens next.

Escalation is deliberately \emph{not} model-driven. A systolic reading
$\geq 160$ or diastolic $\geq 100$ escalates immediately; a reported cost barrier
or side effect escalates after two occurrences within fourteen days. Language
models structure, classify and summarise; they never diagnose and never decide
escalation. That boundary is a medical-safety requirement, and it is what makes
this benchmark meaningful: because the decision rule is fixed and identical
across systems, any difference in escalation outcome is attributable to the
transcript alone.

The corpus contains a deliberate minimal pair. \twi{``Me BP yɛ one-sixteen over
seventy-eight''} is 116/78 and green; \emph{``one-sixty over one hundred''} is
160/100 and red. One vowel separates a routine reading from an immediate
escalation.

\section{Data}

\textbf{Source.} Twenty-one scripted scenarios, written to cover the intents the
agent actually handles: adherence confirmation, cost barrier, forgetting, side
effect, running out, blood-pressure reporting, booking, rescheduling and refills.
The scenario file is the single source of truth from which the scoring reference
is generated; hand-editing the reference is prohibited, because text that drifts
from what speakers read makes every WER figure meaningless.

\textbf{Languages.} Three sets: \textbf{E}, five Ghanaian-accented English
controls; \textbf{T}, nine Twi--English; \textbf{P}, five Pidgin--English.
Numerals and drug names remain in English in every set --- this is what Ghanaian
speakers do, and it is precisely the code-switch boundary under test. Drug names
(\emph{amlodipine}, \emph{lisinopril}, \emph{nifedipine}) and blood-pressure
values therefore sit inside Twi or Pidgin matrix sentences.

\textbf{Size.} Three consented speakers, recorded on personal handsets, giving
\textbf{57 recordings totalling 5.3 minutes} (0.088\,h; mean utterance 5.6\,s),
scored across four configurations for \textbf{228 transcriptions}. Coverage is
uneven --- 28, 19 and 10 recordings per speaker --- and splits 15 English,
27 Twi--English, 15 Pidgin--English; 48 quiet and 9 with ambient noise. Three
calls failed with network \texttt{ConnectionError}s and are excluded from their
aggregates. This is a small corpus and no absolute figure here should be read as
a general claim about Ghanaian speech; what it supports is a controlled
comparison, since every configuration receives identical audio, preprocessing,
normalisation, scoring code and decision rules.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Audio arrived in mixed encodings --- some handsets
produce Opus in an \texttt{.m4a} container, others AAC --- and one API rejects
the former outright. All audio is transcoded to 16\,kHz mono AAC before scoring
so every system receives identical input. We report this because an earlier run
failed on every recording from two speakers for exactly this reason and would,
uninspected, have been written up as catastrophic model failure rather than a
container mismatch. Reference and hypothesis text are normalised identically
before scoring: lower-cased, punctuation stripped, number words converted to
digits, and blood-pressure notation canonicalised so that \texttt{142/95} and
``142 over 95'' collapse to the same form.

\section{Models}

\textbf{Intron Sahara} is an African-built ASR API whose documented input
languages include Twi (\texttt{tw}), Akan (\texttt{ak}), Ghanaian Pidgin
(\texttt{pcm}) and Ga (\texttt{gaa}). We evaluate two configurations:
\textbf{sahara}, sending the set's non-English code, and \textbf{sahara\_en},
pinning the English model regardless of what is spoken.

\textbf{Cartesia Ink} (\texttt{ink-whisper}) is a latency-optimised commercial
ASR service, accessed over the network with an API key. \textbf{faster-whisper}
(\texttt{base}) is the only system here that is not a hosted service: it runs
Whisper's openly released weights locally on CPU, with no key and no network.

Probing each endpoint with every language code, rather than trusting
documentation, produced the cleanest result in the study. Cartesia returns an
explicit \texttt{HTTP 400: invalid language} for \texttt{tw}, \texttt{ak},
\texttt{pcm} and \texttt{gaa} while accepting \texttt{en} and \texttt{sw}: the
only African language it takes is Swahili. Whisper's languages contain none of
the four. Of the systems tested, only the African-built one can be told what
language a Ghanaian patient is speaking.

Documented is not the same as shipped, and we state this plainly. Intron
confirmed that the \emph{Akan--English code-switch pair} --- the configuration
these utterances actually require --- had not shipped at the time of testing, and
was rolling out in the week of 10 August 2026.

\section{Evaluation metrics}

We report \textbf{WER} and \textbf{CER}. CER matters here because code-switched
transcripts are frequently right about content and wrong about word boundaries;
WER charges a full error for a token split that CER shows to be a near miss.

We then score what the agent does with the transcript. \textbf{BP extraction
accuracy} asks whether the systolic/diastolic pair recovered matches the
reference. \textbf{Escalation correctness} --- our headline --- asks whether the
transcript drives the same green/amber/red decision under the fixed clinical
rule; it is the only metric corresponding to a patient outcome. \textbf{Intent
accuracy} asks whether the agent takes the right action. The intent scorer is
rule-based and identical for every system, so it is a fair comparison even where
it is an imperfect absolute measure.

These metrics are appropriate here because the system is autonomous between the
patient and the nurse. No human reads the transcript before an escalation is
raised or withheld, so transcript-level metrics alone cannot establish whether
the system is safe.

Latency is not reported. A second benchmark run shared Sahara's
30\,requests/minute limit during this execution, so per-call timings measure
contention rather than model speed; publishing them would mislead.

\textbf{The English control.} Three of four configurations reach 100\% escalation
correctness on Ghanaian-accented English (Table~\ref{tab:task}); the offline
model reaches 67\%. Because the hosted systems are equivalent here, their
degradation on the code-switched sets is attributable to code-switching rather
than to accent, recording conditions or scoring code.

\textbf{Code-switch penalty} (mean WER on code-switched audio minus WER on the
English control): $+0.387$ for Sahara\,\texttt{(en)}, $+0.510$ for
Sahara\,\texttt{(tw/pcm)}, $+0.521$ for Cartesia, $+0.611$ for faster-whisper.
Every system degrades; the African-built model under an English hint degrades
least.

\textbf{Noise.} Sahara is markedly the most robust. Its WER is essentially
unchanged from quiet to noisy (0.506 to 0.504; 0.417 to 0.405 under the English
hint), while Cartesia degrades from 0.406 to 0.717 and faster-whisper from 0.557
to 0.760. For a clinic waiting room this matters more than a headline average.

\textbf{Escalation failures by direction.} Aggregate accuracy hides a clinically
important asymmetry. Across 57 utterances, Sahara\,\texttt{(tw/pcm)} produced 11
escalation flips, \textbf{five of them missed escalations} --- a reference-red
reading that did not escalate --- and no false alarms. Sahara\,\texttt{(en)}
produced 3 flips (1 missed, 1 false alarm), Cartesia 5 flips (0 missed, 1 false
alarm) and faster-whisper 7 flips (2 missed). These are not equivalent harms: a
false alarm costs a nurse a phone call; a missed escalation is a reading of
160/100 or above that never reaches anyone.

\section{Qualitative findings}

\textbf{Sahara \texttt{(tw/pcm)}: excellent on Pidgin, silent on Twi.} The
aggregate hides two opposite behaviours. With the \texttt{pcm} hint this is the
best system in the study on Pidgin--English, and the only one that preserves
Pidgin rather than Anglicising it:

\begin{quote}\small
\textsc{ref}\ \ \emph{``I no fit buy the medicine, e cost too much for me this month.''}\\[2pt]
\textsc{sahara(pcm)}\ \ ``I no fit buy the medicine, e cost too much for me.''\\[2pt]
\textsc{cartesia}\ \ ``I know if you buy the medicine, it costs two months for me\ldots''\\[2pt]
\textsc{f-whisper}\ \ ``I don't feel bad about medicine, it costs too much\ldots''
\end{quote}

\noindent
Cartesia's rendering inverts the meaning: ``I no fit buy'' (I cannot afford)
becomes ``I know if you buy''. The cost barrier --- the single most important
signal this product detects --- is destroyed. Sahara keeps it, and scores 93\%
intent on Pidgin against Cartesia's 47\%.

With the \texttt{tw} hint the same API returns an \textbf{empty transcript on 16
of 27 Twi--English utterances}, and on 0 of 15 Pidgin ones. It is not mishearing
the English fragments; it does not emit them. Since those fragments carry the
numerals and drug names, every downstream metric collapses: 0\% BP, 0\%
escalation, 4\% intent.

\textbf{Sahara \texttt{(en)}: the best all-round configuration.} Pinning English
recovers Twi--English to 78\% escalation correctness and gives the best overall
CER (0.240) and escalation (83\%). It pays for this on Pidgin intent (53\%
against 93\% under \texttt{pcm}), because the English model normalises Pidgin
into standard English and the rule-based intent detector loses its keywords.

\textbf{Cartesia Ink: strongest on English, weakest on Pidgin.} Best English WER
(0.071) and no missed escalations, but it cannot be told a Ghanaian language
exists, and on Pidgin it falls to 50\% BP and 47\% intent. It also degrades most
under noise. A representative failure: \emph{``I check my BP for house''} became
\emph{``I checked my BB for halves''}.

\textbf{faster-whisper: weakest, but free and offline.} Highest WER and CER in
every set, and it drops digits --- on one Pidgin utterance it rendered 138/89 as
``38 over 89'', silently losing the leading digit of a systolic reading. It is
nonetheless the only configuration that runs with no key, no network and no cost.

\textbf{The minimal pair.} On the 116/78 utterance no system recovered both
numbers; on the 160/100 utterance three of four recovered it exactly. The green
reading is the harder one, and errors there manufacture escalations rather than
suppress them.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{The language hint dominates the model choice --- in both directions.} The
largest effect in this study is not which system was used but what it was told,
and the sign of that effect flips between the two Ghanaian languages. The
\texttt{pcm} hint takes Sahara from mid-table to best-in-study on Pidgin. The
\texttt{tw} hint takes the same API to zero on Twi. A benchmark that tested one
hint, or averaged the two languages together, would have reported neither.

This reframes the result for practitioners: the right deployment is not one model
but a per-language routing policy, and choosing it requires exactly the
per-language task-success measurement reported here.

\textbf{WER and task success disagree in both directions.} In an earlier run
Sahara scored worst on WER until we inspected the transcripts: it had heard
\emph{``one forty-two over ninety-five''} and written \texttt{142/95},
recognising a blood pressure, while others wrote \texttt{142 over 95}. Our
normaliser counted one token against three, and WER punished the system that had
understood best; after canonicalising BP notation it moved from worst to best,
with nothing about the audio or models changed. The converse also occurs: we
observed \emph{``medicine''} transcribed as \emph{``men's son''} --- badly wrong
by any string metric --- while the cost intent was still detected and the patient
routed correctly. CER exposes part of the gap that WER hides, which is why we
report both.

\textbf{Offline operation degrades but does not collapse.} The open-weights
configuration is weakest on every metric, yet still recovers the correct
escalation on 61\% of utterances with no key, no network and no cost. That is not
good enough to be the primary path, but it is the right floor: when a clinic's
connection drops, the agent keeps making correct decisions on most messages
instead of none.

\section{Ethics, inclusion and limitations}

All utterances are scripted; no real patient data was used. Written consent was
obtained from every speaker before recording, with a separate opt-in for
releasing audio. Speakers are identified only by code, never by name. Voice notes
in the production system are stored under generated filenames.

The corpus is small --- three speakers, 5.3 minutes --- and unevenly distributed
(28/19/10 recordings), so the aggregate is weighted towards one speaker and
speaker effects cannot be fully separated from system effects; the
escalation-flip counts rest on modest denominators. The rule-based intent scorer
is tuned to these scenarios and flatters every system equally; it measures
relative performance, not absolute agent quality. The Twi scripts were drafted
and revised by non-native speakers and carry outstanding review questions, so
native-speaker sign-off is required before these figures are cited as a general
claim about Twi ASR. We would rather state these limits than have a reader find
them.

\section{Conclusion}

Three results carry beyond this corpus. The language hint given to a model
changed downstream accuracy more than the choice of model did, and in opposite
directions for Twi and Pidgin --- so the deployable answer is a per-language
routing policy, not a single winner. WER can rank the most clinically useful
transcript last and can penalise errors that change nothing, while escalation
correctness was never fooled. And escalation errors must be reported by
direction, because a missed escalation and a false alarm are not equivalent
harms.

The practical implication is that code-switching must be evaluated explicitly. A
system that performs well on monolingual Twi and well on monolingual English can
still fail on the utterances patients actually produce --- and fail in the
direction that matters, by dropping the numbers.
