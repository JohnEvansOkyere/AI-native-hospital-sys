## Why we're doing this

We're building a health service that lets patients in Ghana talk to a clinic
using WhatsApp voice notes — so someone can just speak, in their own words,
instead of typing English.

The problem is that the speech-recognition systems built by big technology
companies are almost never tested on the way Ghanaians actually speak. Real
people mix Twi, Pidgin and English in one sentence — *"Sika nni hɔ nti, I can't
afford the lisinopril"* — and these systems often get it wrong.

Getting it wrong matters here. If a patient says their blood pressure is
**one-sixty** and the system hears **one-sixteen**, a person who needs a nurse
urgently gets told everything is fine.

**Your recordings are how we test this.** You'll read short lines the way a real
patient would say them on the phone, and we measure which systems understand you
and which don't. Nobody has properly measured this for Ghanaian speech before,
so what you record genuinely matters.

Nothing you record is real health information. It's all made-up clinic messages.
Your name is never used — you're identified only by a code like `S01`.

## How to record — step by step

### Step 1 — Set up

- Use your **phone's normal voice recorder app**. Nothing special.
  (This is on purpose — real patients use ordinary phones.)
- If the app asks what format, choose **WAV** or **M4A**. If it doesn't ask,
  don't worry about it.
- Find a quiet room. Close the door. Turn off the fan and TV.
- Hold the phone like you're on a call, or about an arm's length from your mouth.

### Step 2 — Record each line separately

**One line = one recording = one file.**

Please don't record everything in one long file. It's the most common mistake and
it means we can't use the session.

For each line:

1. Press record
2. **Wait one second, then speak**
3. **Wait one second after your last word, then press stop**

That waiting is important. If the recording stops too early it cuts off your last
word, and you can't hear that it happened without playing it back. It looks like
the computer failed when really the recording did.

### Step 3 — How to say the lines

Say them **the way you would really talk to a nurse at a clinic on the phone.**

- Normal speed, normal voice
- Don't read like a newsreader
- Don't over-pronounce or be extra clear

If it sounds like ordinary talking, it's right.

Two more things:

- **Say numbers as words**, exactly as written — "one forty-two over ninety-five"
- **Keep the medicine names in English** — lisinopril, amlodipine, nifedipine —
  even inside a Twi or Pidgin sentence. That's how people really say them.

### Step 4 — If something goes wrong

- **Stumbled or coughed?** Just record that one line again. No problem.
- **Said it slightly differently and it sounded more natural?** That's fine —
  keep it. But please **write down exactly what you said** so we know. There's a
  space for this at the end.

### Step 5 — Play back a few

Before you finish, play 2 or 3 of your recordings. Check you can hear yourself
clearly and the last word isn't cut off.

## Naming your files

This part matters a lot. Files that aren't named correctly get missed
automatically, so a typo means a lost recording.

### The pattern

```
YourCode_LineID_quiet
YourCode_LineID_noisy
```

### Easy mistakes to avoid

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right | |
|---|---|---|
| `S1_E01_quiet` | `S01_E01_quiet` | two digits |
| `S01_E1_quiet` | `S01_E01_quiet` | two digits here too |
| `S01_t06_quiet` | `S01_T06_quiet` | capital letter |
| `S01 E01 quiet` | `S01_E01_quiet` | underscores, no spaces |
| `S01_E01_Quiet` | `S01_E01_quiet` | all lowercase |
| `S01_E01_quiet.m4a.m4a` | `S01_E01_quiet.m4a` | phones sometimes add this when renaming — check |
